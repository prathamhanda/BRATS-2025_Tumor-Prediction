Abstract
Pediatric brain tumors represent one of the most challenging diagnoses in pediatric oncology, requiring accurate segmentation for treatment planning and monitoring. These tumors exhibit unique imaging characteristics, including small enhancing components and high anatomical variability, as demonstrated in the BraTS-PED 2025 challenge dataset comprising 438 multi-parametric MRI cases (260 training, 91 validation, 87 test). While manual annotation is time-consuming, subject to inter-observer variability, and limited by the availability of specialized radiologists, deep learning-based automated segmentation offers an optimized alternative. The proposed nnU-Net v2 framework employs pediatric-specific preprocessing with brain-masked z-score normalization, extensive data augmentation, automated intensity-based label mapping, and connected-component filtering. The method achieves test set Dice scores of 0.882 ± 0.103 (whole tumor), 0.542 ± 0.380 (tumor core), and 0.590 ± 0.374 (enhancing tumor), with Hausdorff distances of 6.73 mm (WT), 59.07 mm (TC), and 66.67 mm (ET). The framework extracts tumor parameters, including volumes (cm³) for all regions, maximum cross-sectional areas with slice indices, centroids in voxel and millimeter coordinates, bounding box extents, and connected component counts, providing quantitative biomarkers for treatment planning and monitoring. To enhance clinical interpretability and surgical planning, segmented tumors were reconstructed into patient-specific 3D surface models and fabricated using 3D printing. Volumetric discrepancies between digital and printed models were 3–5%, and linear dimensional errors were below 2 mm, confirming geometric fidelity.

1 Introduction
Pediatric brain tumors present fundamentally different challenges compared to adult gliomas in medical imaging, requiring accurate segmentation to guide surgical planning, radiation therapy targeting, and treatment monitoring. Unlike adult cases, pediatric tumors exhibit smaller and often absent enhancing components (present in only 73.8% of cases), higher inter-patient anatomical variability, distinct contrast-enhancement patterns, and inconsistent annotation protocols across clinical datasets [1,2]. These unique characteristics make pediatric tumor segmentation particularly challenging and prone to class imbalance and label noise.
Multi-parametric magnetic resonance imaging (mpMRI) is the standard non-invasive modality for pediatric brain tumor assessment, typically comprising T1-weighted (T1), contrast-enhanced T1-weighted (T1ce), T2-weighted (T2), and T2-weighted fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) sequences [3]. Manual segmentation by expert radiologists is time-intensive, subject to inter-observer variability, and dependent on specialist expertise. These challenges are particularly pronounced in pediatric neuro-oncology, where tumor presentations are heterogeneous and less common [3].

Recent advances in deep learning have demonstrated exceptional capabilities in automating medical image segmentation tasks. The nnU-Net framework [4] has emerged as an efficient baseline for biomedical image segmentation, automatically configuring preprocessing pipelines, network architectures, and training procedures for new tasks. However, pediatric applications face distinct obstacles: enhancing tumor components are frequently very small (<1,000 voxels) or absent, label assignments vary across institutions with different numerical indices for identical tissue classes, and standard adult preprocessing strategies do not transfer directly to pediatric populations. This work addresses these challenges through preprocessing adapted for pediatric MRI, automatic label standardization based on image intensities (avoiding manual re-annotation), and anatomy-aware post-processing to improve prediction quality, achieving competitive performance on BraTS-PED 2025 with efficient training (16 hours) and inference (20 seconds per case).
2 Related Work
Deep learning approaches to brain tumor segmentation have evolved from manually engineered U-Net variants toward automated and task-adaptive frameworks. Raza et al. developed GARU-Net, incorporating gated attention mechanisms and GELU activations, achieving Dice scores of 0.860–0.908 on BraTS 2020 adult glioma data [5]. The introduction of nnU-Net [4] marked a shift from architecture-centric innovation to automated configuration, where preprocessing, network design, and training schedules are adapted to dataset characteristics, establishing it as the standard baseline across BraTS challenges.
Building upon this foundation, recent BraTS-PED 2025 studies have emphasized pediatric-specific optimization and ensemble approaches. Yi et al. demonstrated that integrating nnU-Net, Swin UNETR, and frequency-aware decomposition (HFF-Net) improves segmentation of small subregions, achieving Dice scores of 72.3% (ET), 95.6% (NET), and 92.3% (WT) [6]. Beyond just model backbone design, Capellán-Martín et al. showed that radiomic-guided, lesion-aware ensemble strategies with adaptive cross-validation and structured post-processing can yield competitive performance across BraTS 2025 tasks without reliance on a single architecture [7]. Similarly, Li et al. proposed targeted nnU-Net refinements, including residual encoders with attention mechanisms and specificity-driven regularization, achieving top validation performance on BraTS-PED 2025 and highlighting the importance of pediatric-focused architectural adjustments [8].
Kazerooni et al. established the BraTS-PED challenge [1,2], assembling the largest annotated pediatric brain tumor dataset to date (438 cases) with systematic inter- and intra-rater variability analysis. Their findings underscored key pediatric-specific challenges: enhancing tumor (ET) regions are substantially smaller than in adults, with 26.2% of cases containing no enhancing tissue, and label index inconsistencies across institutions complicate supervised training. Furthermore, standard preprocessing strategies developed for adult gliomas often fail to generalize due to variable skull-stripping quality and extreme class imbalance, with ET frequently representing less than 0.1% of total voxels.
3 Methods

3.1 Dataset
The BraTS-PED 2025 dataset contains 438 multi-parametric MRI scans from pediatric patients with brain tumors, distributed across 260 training cases, 91 validation cases, and 87 test cases. Each case comprises four co-registered MRI sequences: T1-weighted (T1), contrast-enhanced T1-weighted (T1ce), T2-weighted (T2), and T2-weighted Fluid Attenuated Inversion Recovery (T2-FLAIR). All images are skull-stripped and resampled to 1.0 × 1.0 × 1.0 mm isotropic spacing.
Manual annotations delineate tumor subregions using labels {0, 1, 2, 3, 4}, where 0 represents background and 1-4 represent tumor tissue classes. However, the semantic assignment of these numerical labels to specific tissue types (enhancing tumor, necrotic core, edema) was not standardized, requiring automated inference. The dataset exhibits substantial biological heterogeneity: tumor volumes range from 0.5 cm³ to 400+ cm³, enhancing components are absent in 26.2% of cases, and tumor locations span all brain lobes with varying proximity to critical structures.
3.2 Preprocessing and Augmentation
All imaging volumes undergo standardized preprocessing before network training. First, modalities are resampled to a common median voxel spacing to handle resolution heterogeneity typical in pediatric acquisitions. Intensity normalization is performed per-modality per-subject using z-score standardization:
x̂ = (x − μ_mask) / (σ_mask + ε)
where μ_mask and σ_mask are computed strictly within the non-zero brain mask region to avoid bias from background voxels. This conservative brain mask is created by combining all non-zero voxels from the four modalities. To prevent overfitting on the pediatric dataset (260 training cases), on-the-fly data augmentation is applied during training using the batchgenerators framework. Augmentation includes random 3D rotations, elastic deformations, anisotropic scaling (0.85× to 1.25×), gamma intensity changes, and Gaussian noise, applied randomly during each training epoch.

3.3 Intensity-Based Label Mapping
The dataset contained labels {0, 1, 2, 3, 4} with ambiguous tissue assignments that varied across contributing institutions. To identify the numerical label corresponding to each tissue type without manual expert re-annotation, an automated mapping procedure was developed that exploits the distinctive MRI intensity patterns of different tumor tissues.
The procedure samples N = 20 training volumes and computes the mean intensity for each label after within-brain z-score normalization. The key discriminative feature is the contrast enhancement signal, calculated as the difference between T1ce and T1 intensities:
Δ_CE(ℓ) = Z_T1ce(ℓ) − Z_T1(ℓ)
where Z_mod(ℓ) denotes the mean z-scored intensity of label ℓ in the given modality. Enhancing tissue exhibits a strong positive Δ_CE due to gadolinium uptake on contrast-enhanced images, whereas necrotic tissue shows low intensity in both sequences. Based on these intensity signatures combined with volume priors, labels are categorized:
• Enhancing Tumor (ET): label with highest median Δ_CE (example: raw label 1)
• Peritumoral Edema (ED): label with highest FLAIR intensity and larger volume (example: raw label 4)
• Necrotic / Non‑enhancing Core (NCR/NET): remaining tumor labels with low Δ_CE (example: raw labels 2 and 3)

The inferred mapping is expressed in two steps to avoid label confusion:
1) **Raw → BraTS semantic codes**: map raw dataset IDs to the BraTS semantic label set {0,1,2,4} where {0: background, 1: NCR/NET, 2: ED, 4: ET}. In our sample run, the notebook proposed {0→0, 1→4, 4→2, and {2,3}→1}.
2) **BraTS semantic → nnU‑Net consecutive codes**: nnU‑Net requires consecutive labels {0,1,2,3}. Therefore we apply a deterministic conversion **4→3**, meaning **ET is encoded as label 3 during training/inference/post‑processing**, while remaining semantically the enhancing tumor region.

Because raw label IDs may vary across contributing institutions, the mapping is derived from MRI intensity signatures rather than assuming fixed label meanings.

3.4 Network Architecture and Training
The nnU-Net v2 framework automatically configures a 3D U-Net architecture based on dataset properties, including patch size, batch size, and network depth. The model uses a Dice-cross-entropy combined loss function and is trained for 1000 epochs with a batch size of 2. The 3d_fullres configuration was trained on an NVIDIA H100 GPU for 16 hours. Predictions from the five cross-validation folds were used to produce the final results. Where fold outputs overlap, the majority voting is applied; otherwise, the predictions are aggregated over the union of cases. 
3.5 Post-Processing
An optional post-processing step, connected-component filtering is applied to remove anatomically implausible regions. Two operations are performed in sequence (in the **nnU‑Net consecutive label space** where ET is encoded as 3). The whole tumor mask M_WT = 𝟙[y > 0] is first decomposed into 3-D connected components using 26 connectivity, retaining only the largest component. This eliminates spatially disconnected false-positive islands caused by skull-stripping residuals or intensity artifacts. Small isolated regions of predicted enhancing tumor (M_ET = 𝟙[y = 3]) containing fewer than τ = 20 voxels are discarded and relabeled as background.
3.6 Tumor Parameter Extraction
Beyond segmentation masks, the framework extracts clinically relevant tumor biomarkers for treatment planning and monitoring. Volumes are computed from voxel counts and NIfTI header spacing information, converted to cm³ (1 cm³ = 1000 mm³). The following parameters are extracted:
• Volumes (cm³): WT, TC, ET, ED, NCR, and NET computed from voxel counts × voxel volume
• Volume Ratios: ET/WT and TC/WT ratios for tumor composition assessment
• Maximum Cross-Sectional Area: Largest axial slice area (cm²) and corresponding slice index for WT
• Centroids: Tumor center-of-mass in both voxel coordinates and millimeter coordinates
• Bounding Box Extent: 3D extent in millimeters (width × height × depth) for radiation field planning
• Connected Component Counts: Number of spatially disconnected tumor regions for WT and ET
3.7 Evaluation Metrics
Segmentation performance is evaluated using the Dice similarity coefficient (DSC) and 95th percentile Hausdorff distance (HD95) for three composite regions following BraTS conventions:
• Whole Tumor (WT): All tumor tissue and edema (y > 0)
• Tumor Core (TC): Necrotic core and enhancing tumor (y ∈ {1, 3})
• Enhancing Tumor (ET): Enhancing components only (y = 3)

Note: The above TC/ET definitions are written in the **nnU‑Net consecutive label encoding** after the deterministic conversion 4→3. In BraTS semantic codes, ET is 4.
The Dice coefficient measures volumetric overlap between prediction and ground truth. HD95 quantifies boundary distance errors, particularly relevant for radiation therapy planning, where dose margins must account for segmentation uncertainty. When both prediction and ground truth are empty for a region, Dice is defined as 1.0; when exactly one is empty, Dice is 0.0.

4.3 Tumor Parameter Analysis
Figure 2 shows an example case (BraTS-PED-00001-000) with extracted tumor parameters. The nnU-Net prediction yields: WT volume = 83.70 cm³, TC volume = 13.83 cm³, ET volume = 6.84 cm³, and ED volume = 69.87 cm³. Volume ratios are ET/WT = 0.082 and TC/WT = 0.165, indicating a predominantly edematous tumor with minimal enhancing components (10 connected ET components). The maximum axial WT cross-sectional area is 2672.0 mm² at slice index z=96. The WT bounding box extent is 67.0×81.0×59.0 mm, providing radiation field dimensions.
Comparison with the best baseline (z-score outliers) shows substantial differences: baseline WT volume = 124.10 cm³ (48% overestimation), TC volume = 73.31 cm³ (430% overestimation), demonstrating that heuristic methods produce clinically unreliable tumor measurements. These quantitative biomarkers enable objective assessment of tumor burden, treatment response monitoring through longitudinal volume changes, and automated radiation therapy field planning based on bounding box extents.
4.4 Physical Validation
Segmentation outputs for a subset of cases were reconstructed into patient-specific 3D surface models and fabricated using fused deposition modelling. Comparison of physical print measurements against MRI-derived digital models confirmed geometric fidelity, with volumetric discrepancies of 3--5% and linear dimensional errors below 2 mm across all evaluated cases.
5 Discussion
The framework was designed around the specific properties of pediatric MRI rather than adapted from adult glioma pipelines. Brain-masked z-score normalization excludes background voxels that would skew intensity statistics under variable skull-stripping quality. The intensity-based label mapping exploits the known biophysical contrast between gadolinium-enhancing and necrotic tissue to resolve inconsistent label assignments across institutions, eliminating the need for manual re-annotation. nnU-Net v2 was chosen because it automatically adapts to class imbalance, which is critical given that enhancing tumors can represent less than 0.1% of total voxels.
Whole tumor segmentation (Dice 0.882, HD95 6.73 mm) falls within the 5 to 10 mm planning margins used in pediatric radiotherapy, making it clinically usable with selective review. Lower performance on tumor core and enhancing tumor (Dice 0.542 and 0.590) reflects three structural challenges: small subregion size, ill-defined boundaries, and complete absence of enhancing tissue in 26.7% of cases, a ceiling on Dice that is independent of model quality. Ensemble methods such as Yi et al. achieve higher scores but require multiple architectures; the single-model approach prioritises deployability and transparency over marginal performance gains.
Extracted biomarkers, including volumes, ratios, centroids, and bounding boxes, provide structured measurements for radiotherapy planning and longitudinal monitoring without additional manual input. Physical validation via 3D-printed models confirmed geometric fidelity (volumetric error 3 to 5%, linear error below 2 mm). Future work should focus on uncertainty estimation to flag low-confidence predictions for radiologist review, and on external validation across independent institutions to confirm generalisability at scale.
6 Conclusion
The proposed study presents an automated framework for pediatric brain tumor segmentation that addresses dataset-specific challenges through intensity-based label mapping, pediatric-adapted preprocessing, and comprehensive tumor parameter extraction. The study achieves competitive performance on BraTS-PED 2025 (WT Dice 0.882, TC Dice 0.542, ET Dice 0.590) with efficient training (16 hours) and inference (20 seconds per case) suitable for clinical deployment. The intensity-based mapping procedure provides a practical solution for handling ambiguous label assignments in multi-institutional datasets without requiring manual re-annotation. Extracted tumor parameters, including volumes, ratios, centroids, bounding boxes, and component counts, provide quantitative biomarkers for treatment planning, therapy field design, and longitudinal response monitoring. Future work should focus on improving subregion segmentation through advanced architectures, implementing uncertainty estimation for clinical decision support, and validating on independent multi-institutional datasets.
